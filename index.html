Summary

Lava tubes on the Moon and Mars are leading candidates for crewed shelters, because a basalt roof blocks ionising radiation and damps the thermal swing at the surface. Orbital imagery has catalogued hundreds of candidate entrances, but every detection ends at the opening. Roof thickness, the shape of the void beneath and whether the roof will stand cannot be seen from above. On Earth, roof thickness has been measured at isolated transects, never continuously along a natural tube.

Here we show that an autonomous aerial robot obtains that measurement in a single flight. With no positioning infrastructure and no command after launch, it reconstructed 300 metres of Raufarhólshellir, a lava tube in Iceland. Its three skylights anchor the interior to an independent surface model and yield a continuous, metre-resolution profile of the rock cover. The cover ranges from a few metres to fourteen and shields against cosmic rays almost everywhere, so the limiting constraint on shelter is structural. The standing spans demand rock strengths within the range measured for jointed basalt. The measurement survives degradation of the surface model to orbital quality, so the workflow transfers to the catalogued skylights of Mars and the Moon.

The surface model is an eBee X RTK photogrammetric survey flown on 28 August 2025 by the Natural Science Institute of Iceland. The interior map comes from the lidar-inertial reconstruction of the robot's own flight. The orbital transfer test degrades the surface model to the posting and vertical noise of HiRISE-, LROC- and CTX-class terrain models and repeats the whole computation.
